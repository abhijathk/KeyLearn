Four ways the bell could read

Every mock below is the real dropdown at its real width, with the data the product actually has. Pick one — or take the parts you want from several and I'll build that.

Clearing is in all four. Each row has its own × and the header carries a “Clear all”. There is no “mark all read” — opening a notification is what reads it, and a control whose only effect is to hide the fact that you have not looked is not worth the row it sits in. Already built.

Now

The date is winning

The timestamp is the first and most prominent thing in each row, so the eye lands on when before what — and at this width it wraps mid-value into “20 Aug,” / “07:23”.

Nothing says which conversation a reply belongs to, who wrote it, or whether it needs an answer. Clicking marks it read and does nothing else — it doesn't open the ticket, which is the one thing somebody clicking a reply notification wants.

Option A

Conversation card

Who wrote it, which conversation, and enough of the reply to decide whether to open it. The time shrinks to a relative stamp on the right, where it stops competing.

A person and the assistant are visibly different — solid initials versus a dashed outline, named role either way. That honesty is already the rule inside the thread; this carries it to the bell.

Needs from the server
Author name, ticket subject, and a body excerpt on the notification. The first two aren't stored today. ~1 migration
Trade-off
Tallest of the four — about three fit before scrolling.

Option B

One row per conversation

Three replies to one ticket collapse into one entry with a count, rather than three rows saying nearly the same thing. The reference and status travel with it.

The status chip is the useful part — “With a person” against “Resolved” tells somebody whether anything is still owed them, which no per-reply list can.

Needs from the server
Grouping by ticket, plus subject and status. no new columns — the ticket already has both.
Trade-off
You lose “what exactly did they say”; the excerpt is only ever the latest.

Option C

Day-grouped timeline

The date moves out of every row and into a header above the group, so the rows carry only a time. Six or seven fit where A fits three.

It reads as a history rather than an inbox — good if notifications will one day cover more than support replies (a finished course, a new certificate, a learner's first perfect run).

Needs from the server
Nothing. no new columns
Trade-off
Least informative per row — no status, no excerpt of the actual reply.

Option D

Actionable card

A on top, with the status chip and explicit buttons on whichever row is unread. “Waiting on you” is the case worth interrupting somebody for, and this is the only option that says so.

Actions only appear on the unread row. Buttons on every row would turn a list into a form, and there is nothing to do on a reply already read.

Needs from the server
Everything A needs, plus ticket status. ~1 migration
Trade-off
Busiest of the four. Two entries fill the dropdown; on a phone, one.

If you want my pick

B, with two things borrowed from A

  1. B's grouping is the real fix. Three notifications about one ticket is the situation the current list handles worst, and no amount of styling a per-reply row solves it.
  2. Take A's author line. “Ruth · KeyLearn” versus “Tab · AI assistant” costs one line and keeps the promise the thread already makes: the assistant is never passed off as a person.
  3. Take A's excerpt. One clamped line of the latest reply is usually enough to decide whether to open it now or after dinner.
  4. All four open the ticket on click — that's a bug fix, not a design choice, and it lands whichever you pick.
  5. Clearing is a delete, not a “dismissed” flag. A notification is a nudge about something that lives elsewhere; once it has been dealt with there is nothing worth keeping. The conversation it pointed at is untouched.
